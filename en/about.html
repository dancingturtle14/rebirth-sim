How it works

Era packs are weighted by each period's approximate regional population (population^0.6, so small eras still get a chance). Each year's death rate combines infant mortality, child mortality, adult background mortality and a Gompertz curve that rises with age, adjusted for class and health; historical events (war, famine, plague) add extra risk in the matching years.

v2: each era's adult mortality is calibrated automatically with tools/calibrate.js (simulating thousands of lives, counting deaths from war, famine, plague and choice events), so the simulated average lifespan lands in the middle of the reference range in the table below. Survival curves, chances of dying before five and cause-of-death shares are all outputs of this model, not raw historical figures. Money amounts are game conversions, for comparison only. Every choice pop-up cites an era statistic and its source (data/stats.js).

All numbers are rough estimates used for game balance, not precise history. Life expectancy at birth in pre-modern societies was typically about 20–35, mainly because so many infants and children died; many who survived childhood lived to 50–60.

v4: Choose an era, be born anywhere

You can only choose the era (16 anchor years from 1000 BCE to 2020). The game draws your birthplace across 32 regions weighted by that year's estimated births (population ÷ life expectancy, a stationary-population approximation), then draws city or countryside from the urbanisation rate, and your family from estimated class structures. Lifespan, child mortality, literacy, GDP per person and fertility all come from that region and era; where no data exist, values are marked "pre-modern estimate". Every region × era cell uses tools/calibrate_world.js to calibrate adult mortality, so the simulated average lifespan (including war, famine and plague events) matches the data.

v4.1: How assumed values and estimates are marked

Every number is labelled with how "solid" it is:

  • No label: comes directly from real data for that region near that year (censuses, van Zanden et al.'s How Was Life?, UNESCO, Gapminder, Maddison 2023, HYDE), or is interpolated between two real observations less than 60 years apart.
  • estimate: anchored on real local data but extrapolated, e.g. interpolated between observations more than 60 years apart, projected from the nearest local observation using the world literacy trend, borrowed from research on pre-modern child mortality (Volk & Atkinson 2013, about 43%), or a world average.
  • assumed value: no usable data exist for that time and place (e.g. literacy in 1000 CE, or life expectancy in Asia before 1800). The game uses a plausible but unverified number just to keep the simulation running.

Historians' and census anchors for literacy: the 1897 Russian Empire census (21%), the 1861 Italian census (about 78% illiterate), US censuses 1870–1890 (NCES), Maggiolo's French marriage signatures (1786–90, 1816–20), Schofield's English marriage signatures (1754, 1840), Dore (Japan around 1870), Rawski (mid-to-late Qing).

v4.1 fixes: Britain/Ireland population no longer back-fills Ireland's 1950 population into earlier years (Maddison/Gapminder's "UK" for 1820–1920 already includes all of Ireland, so it isn't double-counted); Palestine, South Sudan and Serbia are back-projected from their parent region's share before their data begin; New Zealand before 1200 and Trinidad before 900 are treated as uninhabited. Literacy no longer defaults to the world average or 5%. Data points were reviewed in two rounds, by a maintainer and a checker (see the GitHub repo).

v3: The meaning layer

"Then & Now" compares era estimates with data for Hong Kong and the world today (Census and Statistics Department, UN IGME, UN WPP, UNESCO, FAO, UNHCR, WHO). "History cards" record how major events affected ordinary people, each with its source. The birth-vs-choices shares in "What this life taught me" come from the model: it compares 30 random births in the same era with 30 random lives from the same birth. It's a game model, not a social-science finding. Intergenerational status persistence in "Legacy" follows Clark, The Son Also Rises (2014). Your reflections are saved only on your own device.
